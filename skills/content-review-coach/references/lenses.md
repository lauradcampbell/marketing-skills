# What a senior reviewer looks for

The deeper version of the questions in `SKILL.md`. Read a section when you want a richer version of
a question to share with the learner, or concrete language for what the senior reviewer would say.
Company-specific facts (products, buyer, settled product facts, the house persona bar) are in
`house.md`. The examples here are set at Plinth, the fictional company that file describes.

**Internal material.** The section names below ("time-to-product", "silent scope bias", "the
petering-out pattern") are shorthand for you. Never use them with the learner; describe what the
piece is doing instead (`SKILL.md`, Step 5 §3).

The areas fall into four priority tiers:

- **Tier A (upstream gates).** Two qualifying questions about whether the piece should exist as
  written.
  - **Strategic fit.** Does the piece earn its place on this site at all? Does it serve the
    publisher's business: promote (or naturally lead to) its products, attract readers who match its
    buyer, shorten the path from reader to customer? Or would it be more at home somewhere else (a
    bigger vendor's official docs, a training provider, a competitor)? If a piece fails here, the right
    action isn't "edit it". It's "kill it", "repurpose it" or "move it".
  - **Form.** If the form is wrong, line edits can't fix it: the piece has to be rewritten as a
    different kind of piece. Even a well-placed piece needs the right form to land.

  If either gate fails, the editorial questions below are about polishing something that needs more
  fundamental rework.
- **Tier B (applies to nearly every piece).** Structure, audience, funnel, story, jargon, factual
  accuracy. Images belong here too when the piece is a how-to, explainer, use case, customer story or
  product walkthrough, because for those forms missing visuals are themselves a credibility tell.
- **Tier C (apply when relevant).** Self-promotion, time-to-product (how soon the product is named),
  the title as a sales tell, product siloing (multi-product pieces only). Images sit here for thought
  leadership, opinion and pure interviews: useful but not load-bearing.
- **Tier D (use sparingly).** Links.

**A note on factual accuracy.** It's Tier B because confidently wrong content destroys reader trust:
one error and the reader stops trusting the whole piece. **But on this one the skill *prompts the
learner to verify* rather than asserting.** The coach can hallucinate certainty about product facts,
and hallucinated certainty teaches the wrong lesson. Only the settled facts and renamed terms listed
in `house.md` are safe to flag confidently. See Factual accuracy below.

## Strategic fit

**Principle.** Before any editorial question (before form, structure or audience) ask: *does this
piece earn its place on the site that published it?* Editorial quality matters in service of
whether the piece should exist as written. A perfectly written piece can still be wrong for the
publisher. An imperfect piece that genuinely serves the business is worth editing; an imperfect
piece that doesn't is worth killing.

This is what separates *"this piece needs an edit"* from *"this piece doesn't belong on this site."*
The two look identical on the surface (low traffic, no engagement) but need completely different
actions.

**A common use.** A content lead reviewing older pieces with low traffic and no search rankings is
trying to decide: is this piece bad and fixable, good and under-promoted, or does it not belong on
the site at all? Strategic fit is what tells the third case apart from the first two.

A piece on the company's site should serve the company's business. Concretely, one or more of:

1. **It promotes, or naturally leads to, one of our products.** Direct mentions, a product placed
   where it solves the problem the article describes, or framing that surfaces a pain point our
   product addresses. Purely educational content with no product connection is charity to whoever
   owns the topic: it attracts search traffic but doesn't shorten the path to becoming our customer.
2. **It attracts searches that match our buyer.** A piece can educate without naming products and
   still serve the business, *if* the search intent matches our buyer. "How do I stop fee stages
   running over" matches a studio manager; "what is a timesheet" matches students and freelancers,
   who don't buy.
3. **It earns its place another way** (thought leadership, a customer's voice, presence in a
   community). A customer story earns its place by featuring a real customer using our product. An
   interview earns it by surfacing expertise tied to what our product is for.
4. **The publisher has credible authority on the topic the piece claims.** The easiest one to miss.
   A piece can pass all three above and *still* fail if its topic is broader than what the publisher
   actually delivers. A title like *"AI in Architecture: Trends, Tools, and Transformations"*
   claims authority on AI in design generally. If the publisher's real offering is studio admin
   (time, fees, invoices), the body either overpromises (claiming more than the products do, which a
   subject-matter reader will spot) or reads thin (admitting the narrow offering doesn't match the
   broad title, which shows the title was over-claimed). Either way the piece costs the publisher
   trust: *"if this is what they think AI in architecture is, they don't understand the space."*
   The fix is to **narrow the title and topic to the publisher's actual depth**, which usually means
   killing this piece and commissioning a different, narrower one, not editing it.

If a piece does none of these four, it's a candidate for *"this doesn't belong on our site as
written."* The action might be: kill it, repurpose it for a partner or training resource, move it to
another surface, or rewrite it with a different title, audience and scope with strategic fit in mind
from the start. **When topic authority is the specific failure, even "Rewrite from the title"
usually isn't right, because the topic itself is the problem, not the framing. Kill is the cleaner
call, possibly paired with a separate brief for a narrower piece in the same space.**

**Failure modes.**
- **The charity piece.** A general "what is X" article on a topic owned by a bigger vendor in your space or by the industry,
  with no route to our products. The reader who finishes has a complete answer to their search and
  no reason to look at us.
- **The misaligned-audience piece.** Written for beginners (students, solo freelancers) when our
  products sell to the people who run studios. The audience is clearly named; it's the wrong
  audience for this publisher.
- **The wrong-publisher piece.** Content that would land better somewhere else entirely: a bigger vendor's
  official docs, a training provider, a competitor's blog. Maybe good content, wrong *publisher*.
- **The brief-driven mismatch.** An educational, top-of-funnel body bolted to a "book a demo" call
  to action. The brief probably listed both "search traffic" and "demo conversions" as goals.
  Different readers; the piece serves neither fully.

**How this relates to Audience.** Linked but not identical. Audience asks *"who is this written
for?"* Strategic fit asks *"is that the audience the publisher needs to reach?"* A piece can have a
clearly named audience and still fail strategic fit if that audience isn't the buyer. For most
pieces strategic fit comes up *through* the audience question, which is why the audience questions
include the alignment check. But it can fail on its own too: a piece can serve no audience at all
*and* not belong on the site.

**What to say.** *"Set aside whether it's well written for a minute. Does it earn its place on our
site? Does the reader who finishes it have a reason to look at any of our products? Do the searches
it would attract match our buyer? If the answer to both is 'not really', the right call may not be
'edit it'. It may be 'kill it, repurpose it or move it'."*

**Two examples, showing different failures** (full versions in `examples.md`):

**(i) Misaligned audience: "What is a timesheet? A beginner's guide".** No mention of any Plinth
product, written for students and freelancers starting out, with a "book a demo" call to action
bolted on the end. The audience is named (beginners) but beginners aren't who Plinth sells to. The
reader finishes with a complete answer and no reason to look at Plinth. The action isn't "add product
mentions". It's "kill it, or rewrite for a different audience and angle": studio managers, and
specific pains like month-end timesheet chasing or fee stages running over, each of which maps to a
Plinth product.

**(ii) Topic authority: "AI in Architecture: Trends, Tools, and Transformations".** This one *does*
mention Plinth's products, and its audience (studio leaders) *is* Plinth's buyer, so it passes the
alignment check the timesheet piece fails. It fails on topic authority. The title claims AI in
architecture generally; Plinth's products handle studio admin with a little automation. A real
piece on that topic would have to cover generative design, rendering and the major design-software
suites, ground Plinth can't stand on. The body either overpromises or reads thin, and either way
costs trust. **This is not a Rewrite-from-the-title case**: narrowing the title still leaves Plinth
claiming a space it has no depth in. The call is Kill, optionally with a separate brief for a
genuinely narrow piece Plinth *can* own, like "Three admin jobs a studio manager can stop doing by
hand".

**The difference between the two:** in (i) the topic is fine and the audience is wrong. In (ii) the
audience is fine and the topic is over-claimed. (i) might survive a real rewrite for a new audience
on the same topic; (ii) generally can't, because no rewrite saves a topic the publisher can't
authentically own.

## Form

**Principle.** Before analyzing sentences or structure, ask what *form* the piece is trying to be:
profile, magazine interview, how-to, explainer, listicle, ultimate guide, customer story, thought
leadership essay, FAQ. Each has its own conventions. If the form is wrong, smaller edits can't fix it.

**Failure modes.**
- A tidied-up transcript pretending to be a magazine interview. The writer keeps the interviewee's
  words but strips the quotes, so it reads as a flat third-person monologue with no narrator.
- An ultimate guide that's really a product walkthrough. The title promises comprehensive coverage;
  the body introduces the product in paragraph 3 and never leaves it.
- A how-to with the steps buried under cute section headers instead of plain subheadings.
- A customer story with no customer.

**Example.** A filmed interview with a studio founder was meant to become a magazine-style
article. The brief said "key phrases as quotations" and "stay close to the structure of the video".
The first draft was a tidied-up transcript: no direct quotes, no narrator, no form. The reviewer
pointed to published magazine profiles as references. The feedback didn't land, and the piece
eventually had to be rewritten by someone else.

**What to say.** "What form is this trying to be? Can you point to a published piece that has the
form you want? If you can't, the writer probably couldn't either."

## Structure and flow: non sequiturs, repetition, coherence

**Principle.** Good content has a clear arc, and each paragraph earns its place by moving the point
forward. A reader should never think "how did we get here?" or "didn't they already say this?"

**Failure modes.**
- **Non sequiturs.** A paragraph about fee stages followed by one about studio culture with no
  bridge. Each is fine alone; the transition is missing.
- **Repetition in the prose.** The same thing said three times in slightly different words. Content
  marketers are often taught to "reinforce the key message", which becomes "repeat it until the
  reader gives up". Good writers trust the reader.

  **Not all repetition is bad.** Pull quotes, callouts, hero subtitles, key-insight boxes and bullet
  summaries *often* echo the body on purpose. They're **skim-reader aids**, there so a scanner can
  grab the key line without reading the paragraph. Before flagging repetition, ask whether it's one
  of these doing its job. If it is, leave it. If the writer is just saying the same thing three times
  across paragraphs, flag it.
- **Sections that don't earn their place.** An FAQ added because "we should have an FAQ". A
  conclusion that repeats the introduction. A section that exists because the writer couldn't decide
  whether to cut it.
- **Buried structure.** The real three-step walkthrough hidden under cute subheadings ("Let's add
  some efficiency") instead of plain labels ("Step 1: Create the fee stages").

**What to say.** "Read it once with a pencil. Can you draw a line through the main argument from
intro to conclusion? Where does the line break? Where does it double back?"

## Audience clarity

**Principle.** A good piece tells the reader straight away "this is for me". A bad one either
doesn't know who it's talking to, or silently assumes an audience it never names.

**Failure modes.**
- **Silent scope bias.** An article about tracking time that silently assumes architecture studios,
  when the site also sells to interior design studios whose projects and fee structures differ.
  Either narrow the scope explicitly or give examples from both.
- **Abstract plural personas.** "Project managers", "designers and directors", "admins": categories,
  not people. A reader can't picture a category. For use case pages, see **Named role + specific
  scene** below.

**Example.** A blog about structuring projects described fee stages as "the RIBA-style stages every
project follows", an assumption that made sense for architects and none for interior designers, who
often bill a fixed design fee plus procurement. Either narrow the scope or give examples for both.

## Named role + specific scene (the persona bar for use case pages)

**Principle.** Use case pages live or die on persona specificity. But the usual bar for a B2B use
case is **not** "a named individual with a backstory". It's more specific and more achievable than
that. Getting the bar right matters, because a too-high bar ("write it like Maria is a real person
at a 200-person company on a Tuesday morning") misrepresents the house style. Check `house.md` for
the team's bar; the default is below.

**The bar.** A strong use case persona has four things:

1. **A named role, singular.** Not "designers and project managers". Not "admins". One precise
   role: "the studio manager", "the account manager", "the practice director". Singular, specific,
   nameable.
2. **A pronoun for that role.** "She" or "he" in later sentences. A small move that stops the page
   describing a category from above and makes it tell a story about a person.
3. **Specific present-tense scenes.** Put the role inside actions the reader can picture: "On the
   last Friday of the month, the studio manager opens the timesheet report and counts the gaps. She
   has eleven people to chase before she can start billing." Scenes, not abstractions.
4. **The reader addressed as "you".** "Ready to send invoices on the 1st instead of the 10th?" "You
   can see which fee stages are running over before the invoice goes out." The reader is inside the
   page, spoken to, not described from above.

**What the bar is *not*.** Unless `house.md` says otherwise, it isn't "name the person Maria, give
her a company size and a Tuesday-morning backstory". That's a magazine-feature opener. Don't coach
toward it. If a use case clears the four points above, it's hit the bar.

**Failure modes.**
- Abstract plurals ("designers and project managers", "admins", "studio teams") with no singular role
  to anchor on.
- A role that appears once in the intro and then vanishes.
- No "she" or "he" anywhere: the role is described in third-person plural or passive voice.
- No scene: the pains are abstract category statements ("waste time chasing missing data") rather
  than pictures of what the person is doing.
- The reader is never addressed as "you".

**Positive reference.** The "Get timesheets in before month-end billing" use case in `examples.md`
clears the bar: one role ("the studio manager"), a pronoun, specific scenes, the reader addressed
directly.

**Negative reference.** The "Plinth Hours + Plinth Billing for interior design studios" use case in
`examples.md` misses it: the persona is the abstract plural "designers and project managers", with
no pronoun, no singular role, no scene, and the reader is never addressed.

**What to say.** "Scan the page for a named role, singular. Can you find one? If so, does it come
with a 'she' or 'he' and a specific present-tense scene? If not, the persona is abstract, and an
abstract persona is the tell that the page is really about the product, not the reader."

## Whose story do the section headers tell?

**Principle.** One of the fastest tells on any use case page, and many blog articles, is what the
section headers are about. Headers are the page's skeleton: they commit the writer to a structural
choice the body can't undo. Headers that describe *the reader's problems* in *the reader's
language* tell the reader's story. Headers that describe *the product's features* tell the product's
story with the reader painted on.

**How to check.** Read only the headers, in order. Is this a list of the reader's problems or of the
product's capabilities?

**Reader-story headers (good)**, from the timesheets use case in `examples.md`:
- *"Your team logs hours in three different places"*
- *"Month-end billing waits on the last missing timesheet"*
- *"You find out a fee stage ran over after the invoice has gone"*

Every header is something the reader is struggling with, in their own framing. The reader is the
protagonist of every section.

**Product-story headers (failure)**, from the interior design use case in `examples.md`:
- *"Plinth Hours for structured time capture"*
- *"Streamline studio billing with Plinth Billing"*
- *"Ensure accountability with timesheet approval workflows"*
- *"Plinth Hours + Plinth Billing = seamless studio operations"*

Every header is about the product: what it does, provides or equals. The reader is nowhere in the
skeleton. The body can sprinkle persona words around, but the structural choice has been made, and
it's the wrong one.

**What to say.** "Read just the section headers in order. Are they about the reader's problems or
the product's features? If it's the product, the body copy can't rescue the page: the skeleton is
telling the wrong story."

## Funnel placement

**Principle.** Content sits at a stage of the funnel. Top-of-funnel readers are discovering a
problem, middle-of-funnel readers are comparing options, bottom-of-funnel readers are ready to buy.
Each stage needs a different posture. The most common failure is assuming every reader is ready to
buy, so everything is a sales piece and the funnel has no top or middle.

**Failure modes.**
- "The Ultimate Guide to Project Time Tracking for Architects": a top-of-funnel title that
  introduces the product in paragraph 3 and becomes a pitch. Confused positioning: a reader who
  doesn't track time at all yet has no reason to care about one specific time-tracking product.
- A walkthrough with no "why this and not the alternative" first. That's a documentation page, not
  marketing content.
- A piece trying to be top, middle and bottom of funnel at once. It usually manages none of them.

**How to move a piece toward the middle of the funnel (a content strategist's instinct).** Add the
alternatives the reader is actually weighing (for studio billing: a spreadsheet, a general-purpose
invoicing tool, Plinth Billing). Put the comparison before any walkthrough. Make the honest case for
our product, then let the walkthrough be the evidence.

**Example.** A "Studio billing without a finance team" blog was all Plinth Billing walkthrough from
start to finish: a bottom-of-funnel shape. The rewrite request: first cover the options the reader
is really choosing between, say honestly why Plinth Billing fits a studio better, and *then* show
how it works.

## Story: does the article have a point?

**Principle.** Even explainers have a throughline: "here's what you need to know about X, why it
matters, and what to do about it." A list of facts with no story isn't a blog; it's an encyclopedia
entry. For use cases and customer stories the story is more explicit still: before and after,
problem and solution.

**Failure modes.**
- A blog listing five things about a topic without connecting them to a point the reader should take
  away.
- A use case that lists features instead of telling the story of "the studio manager" solving a
  specific problem. (See Named role + specific scene above.)
- A customer story that reads like a brochure with the customer's name pasted in.

**What to say.** "If you had to sum up the point of this piece in one sentence, could you? If not,
the writer probably couldn't either."

## Self-promotion tells

**Principle.** The moment the writer bigs up their own product, the reader knows they're being sold
to. Puffery destroys credibility.

**Failure modes.**
- "This incredible application…"
- "Our awesome devs…"
- "Let's walk you through this amazing…"
- "Powerful features that transform…"
- Any sentence that compliments the product instead of showing it at work.

**The alternative.** Show, don't puff. "Here's what month-end looks like when approved hours flow
straight into the invoice" persuades. "This incredible feature makes billing easy" is a pitch.

**A strategist's line on it:** don't call your own product incredible or say how awesome your
developers are. That's copywriting 101. The moment you start bigging up the product, the reader
knows you're selling something.

## Time-to-product

**Principle.** Count the paragraphs before the product is mentioned. It's a fast proxy for whether
the article has earned the right to pitch. An ultimate guide that introduces the product in
paragraph 3 is really a walkthrough in disguise.

**What to say.** "Read the first five paragraphs. Where does the product appear? Has the reader been
given a reason to care by that point?"

## Jargon and buzzwords (the buzzword list)

**Principle.** B2B marketing has a pile of words that sound smart and say nothing. They're almost
always a sign the writer was trying to sound important rather than be useful. If a sentence contains
one, flag it. If it contains three, the whole sentence needs rewriting.

*Source, for you only:* the list is inspired by Emma Stratton's work on plain B2B messaging. Never
name the source to the learner (`SKILL.md`, Step 5 §3(c)).

**The "don't use" list.** Not exhaustive, but the worst offenders:

- **seamless / seamlessly** (describes nothing)
- **leverage** (almost always means "use", which is a better word)
- **utilize** (always means "use")
- **robust** (what does it actually describe?)
- **scalable** (everyone says it; nobody shows it)
- **innovative** (real innovation shows itself)
- **synergy / synergies** (never specific)
- **streamline** (a generic efficiency word; find a concrete verb)
- **unlock** (unlock value, potential, the power of: empty)
- **empower** (empower your team, your users: empty)
- **best-in-class / best-of-breed** (superlatives with no evidence)
- **game-changer / game-changing** (always a pitch word)
- **cutting-edge** (same)
- **world-class** (same)
- **solution** (used vaguely: "our solution" tells you nothing)
- **enable / enables** (enables users to, enables teams to: an empty connective)
- **journey** (empty unless you're describing a real path)
- **ecosystem** ("a bunch of things that connect", without saying how)
- **holistic** (rarely means anything)
- **transformative / transformation** (empty without the before and after)
- **optimize / optimization** (empty without what's optimized and how)
- **maximize / maximise** (same)
- **drive** (drive results, growth, engagement: an empty verb for "cause")
- **mission-critical** (drama for its own sake)
- **bandwidth** (when it means "time" or "capacity")
- **take it to the next level** (says nothing)
- **value-add / deliver value** (what kind of value?)
- **thought leadership** (usually self-aggrandizing)
- **circle back / touch base** (meeting-speak)
- **move the needle** (says nothing)

**What to say.** "Highlight every sentence with a word from the buzzword list. How many would still
mean anything if you deleted the buzzword? Usually none. The reader isn't getting information,
they're getting vibes, and the vibes say 'I'm a marketer'."

## The title as a sales tell

**Principle.** The title is the first signal of the writer's intent toward the reader. A title that
leads with the product name, or crams in keywords, signals a sales piece before the reader reads a
sentence. A short title framed around value to the reader signals the writer cared about the reader.

**Failure modes.**
- "Plinth Hours – Multi-Currency Support for Timesheets Now Available in Project View": wordy,
  product name first, boring.
- "Plinth Billing – Standard Approach to Creating Stage Invoices with Templates": same.
- Blog titles shouldn't include the product name unless it's essential (a launch announcement, say).

**A strategist's line on it:** who is going to read a blog with a title like that? Titles like these
are wordy and, worse, boring.

## Product siloing vs bundled value

**Principle.** When two or more of our products genuinely work together to solve a problem, the
content should show them working together, not as separate universes. **Check `house.md` first**:
only products listed as working together can be siloed. Presenting incompatible products separately
is just accurate.

**Example.** The interior design use case in `examples.md` features Plinth Hours and Plinth Billing
in separate sections and never shows approved hours turning into an invoice, which is the whole
reason a studio would buy both.

## Link quality

**Principle.** Every link is a promise: "click here and you'll find X." Broken promises destroy trust
fast. Lower priority than structure, story and funnel: check links when they're obviously off, but
don't drive the analysis from them.

**Check.**
- Does the link go where the text says?
- Does the destination actually cover what was promised?
- Is it a current resource, not a five-year-old post?

**Examples.** A "read our guide to fee proposals" callout linking to a blog that never mentions fee
proposals. A "Plinth Rooms" link pointing to a 2021 launch post instead of the current product page.

## Images and screenshots

**Principle.** For anything that teaches, explains or demonstrates (how-tos, explainers, use cases,
product walkthroughs) images aren't decoration; they're load-bearing. A reader can't build a mental
model of "how to set up fee stages" from prose alone, however careful the prose. And missing
screenshots are a credibility tell: they suggest the writer never did the thing, or didn't think the
reader needed to see it.

The question is **"does the balance of text and images match what the piece is trying to do?"** A
thought-leadership essay doesn't need screenshots. "How to set up fee stages in Plinth" absolutely
does.

**Failure modes.**
- **A practical title with no visual payoff.** "What Plinth Billing Can Really Do" with no screenshot
  of an invoice, of the stage view, or of a before and after. The title wrote a cheque the visuals
  didn't cash.
- **A use case that says two products solve a problem but never shows either.** The page asks the
  reader to imagine the solution. Showing it is a stronger pitch.
- **A wall of text on a visual topic.** Workflows, approval chains, project structures are visual
  subjects. A wall of prose is the wrong form.
- **Stock photos instead of product images.** A "smiling woman at laptop" doesn't stand in for a
  screenshot of the feature. If the only images in a practical piece are stock photos, it's
  decorated, not illustrated.

**What to say.** "This is trying to be a practical explainer. Where are the screenshots? A reader who
wants to understand fee stages can't picture the setup from prose alone. The missing visuals are
themselves a tell: either the writer didn't do it, or didn't think the reader needed to see it.
Either way the piece isn't keeping its promise."

**When this matters most:** how-tos, explainers, use cases, customer stories. Less: thought
leadership, opinion, pure-text interviews.

## Factual accuracy (use with caution)

**The area most likely to backfire if used carelessly.**

**Principle.** Factual errors matter, especially product and technical claims. A reader who spots
one stops trusting the whole piece. But **the coach can't always tell whether a claim is true**, and
confidently calling a true claim wrong teaches the wrong lesson and erodes trust in the coach.

**What to do:**
- **Notice** claims that seem questionable or are specific enough to need checking.
- **Prompt the learner to verify** rather than asserting: *"The article says X. I'd want to verify
  that before we flag it. Do you know if it's accurate, or should we check the docs together?"*
- **Be confident only about what `house.md` lists as settled**: renamed terms (flag only when the
  old name actually appears) and settled structural facts.
- **Be cautious about everything else**: structure, permissions, plan limits, feature availability.
- **Watch for credit given to the wrong tool.** A claim that our product does something a bigger vendor
  in your space or a tool you integrate with actually does, or the reverse. Example: a Plinth blog said "Plinth Billing
  works out the VAT on every invoice for you". Per `house.md`, tax is calculated by the studio's
  accounting software after the invoice syncs. That kind of mix-up is common in AI-drafted content
  and worth a careful read. Don't assert it unless it contradicts `house.md`, but it's a smart probe:
  *"Does any of this give our product credit for something the accounting software does, or the
  other way round?"*

**Why this matters.** Real writers do get product facts wrong: which thing contains which, what's
available on which plan. When a senior reviewer who *knows* spots the error, it's teachable. When an
AI "spots" an error it hallucinated, it's worse than missing it: the learner now has wrong
information delivered with confidence.

**What to say.** "Accuracy matters, but I'll be careful here: I don't want to flag something unless
I'm confident. The article says [X]. Does that match what you know, or is it worth checking
together?"

## Traffic as a feedback signal (published pieces)

**Principle.** If the piece is published and analytics are available, real traffic is useful
evidence: not a critique on its own, but confirmation of what the other questions have already
surfaced.

**Example.** The interior design use case in `examples.md` had 9 visits in 8 months. That confirmed
the piece wasn't finding its audience.

**How to use it.** Low traffic is a lagging indicator. Use it to confirm what the structure and story
questions have already shown, not as the main flag.
