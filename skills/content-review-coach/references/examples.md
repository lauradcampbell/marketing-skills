# Worked examples of senior reviews

Illustrations of what a senior reviewer flags, organized by the question they illustrate, so when
you're walking the learner through funnel placement you can draw on the funnel example.

**These examples are invented.** They're set at Plinth, the fictional company in `house.md`, and
each one is modelled on a real kind of review: the same mistake, the same reasoning and the same
fix, with the company, products, people and numbers changed. If you replace `house.md` with your own
company, replace these with real reviews from your own team; they teach better than invented ones.

Like everything in `references/`, this is internal material. Translate it into plain language for
the learner and never cite an example by name (`SKILL.md`, Step 5 §3).

---

## Form

**A founder interview that became a transcript.**
Plinth filmed an interview with Amara, who founded a 30-person interior design studio. The brief
asked for "a magazine interview with key phrases as quotations, staying close to the structure of
the video." The first draft was a tidied-up transcript: no direct quotes, no narrator, the founder's
words rephrased into third person. The reviewer's note: *"I wanted it to read like a magazine
interview. When I said key phrases as quotations, I meant literally: As Amara explained, 'I started
out drawing kitchens for other people's clients, and I kept seeing the same mistakes…'"* The
reviewer linked to published magazine profiles as references. The next drafts didn't change shape,
and the piece was eventually rewritten by someone else.

**The lesson.** When you see a form mismatch, show a reference, don't just describe it. "Make it
like this published piece" is faster and clearer than "make it more like a magazine interview".

## Funnel placement

**"The Ultimate Guide to Project Time Tracking for Architects".**
The title promises comprehensive coverage. A long intro, then Plinth Hours arrives in paragraph 3,
followed by *"Let's walk you through this incredible application."* The three setup steps aren't in
the subheadings; they're buried under "Let's add some efficiency". The strategist's review: *"If a
general time-tracking vendor wrote this, it would be bottom of their funnel. But for a Plinth Hours
blog it's very top of the funnel, because it assumes the reader doesn't track time at all. And if
they don't track time, why would they care about Plinth Hours? Yet more than half the article is
about Plinth Hours. I've no idea where this is meant to sit in the funnel or who it's for, and I
don't think the writer does either."*

**The lesson.** Confused positioning means the writer doesn't know who they're writing to. A
confused funnel is usually a symptom of a confused audience.

## Justify before you demonstrate (moving a piece to the middle of the funnel)

**"Studio billing without a finance team".**
The original was walkthrough from start to finish: how to build a stage invoice in Plinth Billing.
The strategist asked for a rewrite that first covers what studios actually use to bill (a
spreadsheet, a general-purpose invoicing tool) and says honestly why Plinth Billing fits a studio
better, *before* the step-by-step. The rewritten piece acknowledges the choice the reader is making,
makes the case, and lets the walkthrough be the evidence.

**The lesson.** A how-to has to earn its place. Name the alternatives the reader is weighing, make
the considered case, then demonstrate.

## The title as a sales tell

**Two blog titles.**
- *"Plinth Hours – Multi-Currency Support for Timesheets Now Available in Project View"*
- *"Plinth Billing – Standard Approach to Creating Stage Invoices with Templates"*

The strategist: *"Who is going to read a blog with these titles? They're wordy and, worse, they're
boring. Blog titles shouldn't have the product name in unless it's absolutely necessary. It marks the
blog as a sales piece instead of something useful."*

**The lesson.** Wordy plus product name in the title is a sales tell before anyone clicks.

---

## Factual accuracy

**A freelance writer's blog on structuring studio projects.**

The blog claimed or implied:
- Fee stages can contain projects → **Wrong.** A project contains fee stages, never the reverse.
- You can log hours straight to a project → **Wrong.** Hours are logged against a fee stage.
- Any studio can split a project's invoices across two clients → **Not on the standard plan.** It
  needs the Studio plan.
- "An hours entry should never be shorter than 15 minutes" as a rule of thumb → **Not a real rule**
  any studio manager would recognize.

It also used old names throughout: "Jobs" (now Projects), "Timers" (now Hours entries), "Client
portal" (now Client view). And it presented plan-limited features as if every customer had them.

**The lesson.** A reader who spots one factual error stops trusting the piece. Check every product
claim, even the ones that sound reasonable.

## Ground the question in what people actually ask

**The same blog: the made-up question.**
One section was titled *"Should every project have the same number of fee stages?"* The reviewer:
*"This question is a bit strange, because I read a lot of what studio managers ask in support
tickets and on forums, and no one asks this. What they ask is: how do I bill a fixed-fee stage when
we've gone over the hours? Can I move hours from one stage to another after the fact? How do I split
one project across two invoices? Those are the real questions. Nobody's asking about the number of
stages."*

**The lesson.** Ground every section in questions real users actually ask. If nobody asks it on
forums, in support or on sales calls, nobody will search for it either.

## Self-promotion tells

**"Let's walk you through this incredible application".**
The strategist: *"Don't call your own product incredible or say how awesome your developers are.
That's copywriting 101. The moment you start bigging up the product, the reader knows you're selling
something."*

**The lesson.** Watch the words the writer uses about their own product. "Incredible", "awesome",
"powerful", "amazing", "transformative" are pitch words, not explanation words.

---

## Link quality

**The callout that went nowhere useful.**
A blog had a callout, "Read our guide to fee proposals", linking to a post about project templates
that never mentions fee proposals. The reviewer: *"This post doesn't mention fee proposals at all, so
it's really odd. Why is it here? Take it out."*

**The link to an old post.**
A "book rooms without the back-and-forth" callout linked to the 2021 launch post for Plinth Rooms
instead of the current product page. The reviewer: *"If this is a link about Rooms, it should go to
the Rooms product page, not a four-year-old announcement."*

**The lesson.** Click every link. Does it go where the text says? Does the destination deliver what
was promised?

---

## Right product, right feature

**A blog that promoted Plinth Rooms for a billing problem.**
A paragraph about getting invoices out faster at month end promoted Plinth Rooms ("fewer meetings
about money"). The reviewer: *"This is a good paragraph, but the product to promote here is Plinth
Billing, not Rooms. We're talking about invoices going out late. Billing is the product. This
paragraph should show how approved hours turn into an invoice."*

**The lesson.** When you see a product recommendation, ask: does this product actually solve the
problem the paragraph just described? If not, it's promoting the wrong product, and the reader will
notice.

## Positive reference: a use case that clears the bar

**The page.** "Get timesheets in before month-end billing", a Plinth Hours + Plinth Billing use case.

Use it as the **positive calibration point** for use case analysis. When a learner's page is below
this bar, this is the page to compare it with.

**What it gets right, with the text:**

**1. A named role, not a named individual.** The protagonist is "the studio manager". Not "Maria at
a 40-person practice". Singular, precise, easy to picture.

**2. A pronoun.** *"On the last Friday of the month, the studio manager opens the timesheet report
and counts the gaps. **She** has eleven people to chase before she can start billing."* The "she"
stops the page sounding like a category description and makes it a story.

**3. A specific present-tense scene.** *"Hours live in three places: the timesheet, a few
spreadsheets, and the notes app on the project architect's phone. When the client asks why the
concept stage cost more than quoted, the studio manager has to piece it together by hand."* The
reader is inside the workflow.

**4. Reader-story headers.** All three headers state the reader's problem, not the product's
features:
- *"Your team logs hours in three different places"*
- *"Month-end billing waits on the last missing timesheet"*
- *"You find out a fee stage ran over after the invoice has gone"*

The skeleton tells the reader's story. The body confirms it.

**5. The reader addressed as "you".** *"Ready to send invoices on the 1st instead of the 10th?"* /
*"You can see which fee stages are running over while there's still time to talk to the client."*
The reader is spoken to, not described.

**6. A concrete before and after.** Before: chasing timesheets in chat, invoices going out on the
10th, overruns discovered after the client has been billed. After: weekly approval, approved hours
flowing straight into the stage invoice, an overrun warning before billing. The reader knows what's
different about her month.

**7. An honest emotional beat.** *"Every single month."* Three words acknowledging the reader's
feeling. Not a buzzword, not puff, just a human beat.

**The lesson.** This is the bar. When a use case has a named role, a pronoun, specific scenes,
reader-story headers, "you", and a concrete before and after, it's working. When it doesn't, those
are the specific things to name. Don't invent a higher bar (named characters, company sizes, story
openers) the house style doesn't ask for.

## Product siloing

**"Plinth Hours + Plinth Billing for interior design studios".**
The persona is "designers and project managers". Two sections on Hours, two on Billing, and the page
never shows approved hours becoming an invoice, which is the whole reason a studio buys both. The
reviewer's verdict: *"This use case doesn't work. It promotes the wrong features, it doesn't tell the
story of a pain point being solved, and it silos Hours and Billing instead of showing them working
together. We need to scrap it and rewrite it. It's had 9 visits in 8 months."*

**The lesson.** When two products solve a persona's problem together, show them together. Presenting
them separately wastes the reason to buy both.

---

## Traffic as a feedback signal

**Nine visits in eight months.**
The reviewer used the traffic as part of the critique, not separately. A use case that's been live
for eight months with nine visits isn't just underperforming: it's evidence that the piece isn't
finding its audience, which is useful for the rewrite.

**The lesson.** If the piece is published, check the numbers. Low traffic lags, but it confirms what
the other questions have already shown.
